Lecture 15 · 3D vision · Jiajun Wu

Choose a representation for the geometric question you need to answer

A point cloud, mesh, distance field, radiance field, and part graph describe different aspects of a scene. Deep learning does not eliminate these choices: it makes the representation's strengths and blind spots part of the learned system.

Extend inspection from appearance to shape 1:01

A dark mark on our manufactured part may be a scratch, a shadow, or a shallow dent. A single image can be insufficient to distinguish them. We now add depth measurements and several calibrated views, and ask geometric questions: how deep is a recess, is a mounting hole blocked, and will a reconstructed part fit its assembly? These questions demand more than a plausible picture.

The lecture begins with representations before neural architectures. This is a consequential order. A representation determines which queries are cheap, which operations are natural, how much memory is required, and what information is absent. Geometry describes spatial shape; material describes how a surface interacts with illumination; radiance describes light traveling in a particular direction. A system can reproduce radiance convincingly without recovering an accurate manufacturing surface or the underlying material.

Let a spatial query be \(q=(q_x,q_y,q_z)\in\mathbb R^3\), expressed in a stated coordinate frame and unit. An explicit representation supplies points on the object or surface elements connecting them. An implicit representation supplies a function whose values define the shape. This distinction is about how geometry is accessed. It is separate from whether the representation is learned, whether it uses a finite parameter vector, and whether it is stored on a grid.

For the factory case, millimeters versus meters is not a cosmetic convention. A geometric loss, a signed distance, and a rendering density all change meaning with units. Coordinate frames also matter: points from two scanners cannot simply be concatenated before their relative pose is known. Keep transformations and calibration as explicit parts of the data pipeline.

Points and meshes expose surfaces but encode different structure 5:05

A point cloud is an unordered collection \(P=\{p_i\}_{i=1}^N\), usually stored as an \(N\times3\) array. Its row order has no intrinsic geometric meaning. Optional normals \(n_i\) describe surface orientation; oriented surface samples are often called surfels. A normal helps a renderer determine how a light direction meets the surface, but a noisy normal estimate can produce an appearance error even when a point location is accurate.

Depth sensors naturally produce samples, often with noise, occlusion, and nonuniform density. Registering several scans estimates their transformations into a common frame. A sparse region does not imply an empty region: it may have been hidden from every sensor. Point clouds do not inherently specify faces, inside/outside labels, or topology. They support many useful processing algorithms, but those algorithms must infer or introduce structure absent from the raw set.

A triangle mesh stores vertices \(V\in\mathbb R^{N\times3}\) and faces \(F\in\{1,\ldots,N\}^{M\times3}\). Each row of \(F\) indexes three vertices. Connectivity enables operations such as subdivision, simplification, and neighborhood smoothing. It also introduces validity questions: are faces consistently oriented, is the mesh closed, do triangles intersect, and are there nonmanifold edges? Increasing the triangle count cannot fix a wrong topology by itself.

For an original example, consider four points on a square. Listing their coordinates does not tell us whether they represent a filled plate, the rim of a hole, or disconnected samples. Two triangles add a particular surface interpretation. A closed solid mesh adds still more assumptions. Our hole-inspection task therefore cannot infer blockage from point density alone without modeling visibility and surface connectivity.

Parametric surfaces make sampling direct, not necessarily uniform 11:21

A parametric surface maps a two-dimensional domain to3D: \(s(u,v)\in\mathbb R^3\). A unit sphere can be represented by \(s(u,v)=(\sin u\cos v,\sin u\sin v,\cos u)\), with \(u\in[0,\pi]\) and \(v\in[0,2\pi)\). Two parameters suffice because a surface is locally two-dimensional even though it sits in three-dimensional space. Curves similarly need one local parameter.

Bézier curves and surfaces use control points and basis functions to construct smooth shapes. For example, a quadratic Bézier curve is \(b(t)=(1-t)^2p_0+2t(1-t)p_1+t^2p_2\), \(t\in[0,1]\). The nonnegative coefficients sum to one, so the curve lies in the control points' convex hull. Moving the middle control point changes the curve without independently editing hundreds of samples. This helps explain why designed objects often benefit from structured parameterizations.

Evaluating \(s(u,v)\) gives a point on the surface immediately. But uniform random parameters generally do not give uniform surface area. The area element is \(\|\partial_us\times\partial_vs\|\,du\,dv\); for the sphere it is \(\sin u\,du\,dv\). Uniform \(u\) overweights the poles. To sample the sphere uniformly, sample its height uniformly in \([-1,1]\) and its azimuth uniformly, then recover the other coordinates. The distinction matters when a point-cloud loss is supposed to weight surface area rather than the arbitrary parameterization.

By contrast, testing whether an arbitrary point lies inside a general parametric surface may require extra geometric algorithms and a closed-surface interpretation. The lecture's general tradeoff is useful, but special shapes such as spheres have simple inside tests regardless of how one initially wrote them down.

Implicit fields make spatial queries direct 17:32

An implicit surface is a level set \(S=\{q:f(q)=0\}\). Adopt negative values inside and positive values outside. For the unit sphere, \(f(q)=\|q\|_2^2-1\) has the correct zero set and signs, but it is not a signed distance. The signed distance is \(d(q)=\|q\|_2-1\). At radius2, the former returns3 while the latter returns1. Confusing them makes distance thresholds and ray-marching step lengths wrong.

At a regular surface point where \(\nabla f(q)\ne0\), a unit normal is \(n(q)=\nabla f(q)/\|\nabla f(q)\|_2\), with orientation determined by the sign convention. A true signed-distance function has unit gradient magnitude wherever it is differentiable. Corners and points with multiple nearest surface locations require care; differentiability is not guaranteed everywhere.

Signed fields make constructive solid geometry straightforward. With negative-inside fields \(f_A,f_B\), the union is described by \(\min(f_A,f_B)\), the intersection by \(\max(f_A,f_B)\), and the difference \(A\setminus B\) by \(\max(f_A,-f_B)\). These formulas preserve the intended signs and boundaries under the usual solid interpretation. They do not generally preserve exact Euclidean signed distances everywhere. Smooth blends further change geometry rather than merely providing an equivalent representation.

For a hypothetical annular part, let \(d_{\mathrm{outer}}(q)=\|q\|-2\) and \(d_{\mathrm{inner}}(q)=\|q\|-1\). The field \(\max(d_{\mathrm{outer}},-d_{\mathrm{inner}})\) represents a spherical shell. At the origin it is positive: the central cavity is outside the material. At radius1.5 it is negative. At radius2.5 it is positive again. This is an original3D sign test, not a model of an actual scanned component.

Sampling the zero set of a complicated field requires root-finding, meshing, or another procedure. The ability to evaluate a field at any location does not make the surface instantly available. Explicit and implicit representations exchange the difficulty of different operations; neither wins every task.

Grids simplify computation while spending memory on space 23:58

A field can be sampled on a regular3D grid. Interpolation then approximates values between grid nodes, and a zero crossing locates a surface. Opposite signs along an edge imply a crossing for a continuous interpolant, but equal endpoint signs do not rule out an unresolved thin feature between them. Binary occupancy grids retain only inside/outside information, with the bit convention stated explicitly. Voxels can also store other quantities; binary occupancy is the lecture's introductory example.

A dense \(n\times n\times n\) grid uses \(n^3\) cells. One float32 scalar at resolution256 needs \(256^3\cdot4=67{,}108{,}864\) bytes, or64MiB. At512 it needs512MiB. With32 feature channels, these become2GiB and16GiB before gradients or other activations. Doubling linear resolution multiplies volume memory by eight. A volumetric convolution is easy to define from chapter5, but dimensional regularity does not make it cheap.

An octree recursively divides a spatial cell into eight children. It can reserve fine cells for complicated boundaries and use coarse cells in homogeneous regions. This reduces waste when geometry is spatially sparse, at the price of irregular data structures and operations. It is an octree, not an octave tree. Sparse storage does not recover detail that was never measured, and memory gains depend on the shape and refinement policy.

3D supervision and2D supervision answer different questions 29:25

The lecture traces data growth from small shape benchmarks to ShapeNet, Objaverse, object scans, CO3D, part annotations, and scanned indoor scenes. These resources differ in what they contain: artist-created assets, physical scans, multi-view videos, semantic parts, or scene reconstructions. Their raw counts are not directly comparable. A collection of thousands of views can contain only a few hundred distinct objects, and synthetic assets can differ systematically from damaged real factory parts.

This creates a strategic choice. Direct3D supervision can specify geometry, occupancy, or distances. Image supervision instead constrains how a representation renders from observed cameras. The latter can exploit far more image data, but may leave unobserved surfaces and geometry ambiguous. A glossy metal reflection should not be mistaken for a geometric cavity just because both explanations fit one image.

Tasks include shape classification, part segmentation, completion, generation, and novel-view synthesis. Shape completion must infer unobserved structure; it should therefore be evaluated separately from fitting measured points. Novel-view synthesis asks for new images and can succeed without a watertight, dimensionally accurate mesh. For our inspection system, metric fit and collision geometry require geometric validation even if rendered views appear excellent.

Use object-disjoint and scene-disjoint evaluation where appropriate. Splitting adjacent frames of the same scanned part between training and test can make a reconstruction system appear to generalize while it mainly recognizes an existing object. Likewise, do not evaluate synthetic geometry solely on the rendered images used to train it.

Reuse image models or respect point-set symmetry 38:31

A multi-view method renders several images of a shape, extracts features with a shared image network, and pools across views. The primary Multi-view CNN paper develops this route for shape recognition. It makes image pretraining available to3D tasks. Its limitations include incomplete view coverage, sensitivity to rendering choices, and information lost in projection. A hidden defect cannot influence a view that never sees it.

The lecture next discusses volumetric generative models and joint shape/appearance generation. A3D generator can produce voxels, a differentiable projection can produce depth or silhouettes, and an image model can produce appearance. Separate latent variables for shape, texture, and viewpoint can provide control, but their intended meanings must be validated; merely naming variables does not guarantee disentanglement. Octrees improve the efficiency of grid-based routes.

PointNet attacks the irregularity directly. A simplified global descriptor is \[ h(P)=\rho\!\left(\max_{i=1,\ldots,N}\phi(p_i)\right), \] where \(\phi:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^D\) is shared across points, the maximum acts coordinatewise, and \(\rho\) maps the pooled descriptor to the task output. Permuting points permutes their embeddings and leaves the maximum unchanged. This gives exact permutation invariance of the global output. The PointNet paper also treats pointwise tasks; for segmentation the output labels should permute with their input points, a property called equivariance.

Permutation invariance is not full sampling invariance. Removing the only point that attains a feature maximum can change the descriptor. Sum pooling changes when points are duplicated; mean pooling changes with sampling density unless additional assumptions hold. Max pooling ignores duplicate points but can still miss small structures that were never sampled. The lecture presents sampling robustness as a desideratum, not something symmetry alone proves. Neighborhood graphs and hierarchical point methods add local relationships that a simple global pooling construction does not explicitly encode.

A point-cloud loss must specify correspondence and normalization 49:32

For point sets \(P,Q\), define a squared, mean-normalized bidirectional Chamfer loss: \[ L_{\mathrm{CD}}(P,Q)= \frac1{|P|}\sum_{p\in P}\min_{q\in Q}\|p-q\|_2^2 +\frac1{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}\min_{p\in P}\|q-p\|_2^2. \] Other implementations sum, average the two directions, or omit squaring. Report the convention and units. Nearest-neighbor matching is many-to-one: several predicted points may share a nearest reference point. As assignments change, the loss is generally piecewise differentiable, with ties requiring a convention.

For an original one-dimensional example embedded along the3D x-axis, take \(P=\{0,2\}\), \(Q=\{0,3\}\). The two directed mean squared distances are each0.5, so our Chamfer loss is1. For equal-size sets, a one-to-one assignment loss is \(L_{\mathrm{match}}=N^{-1}\min_\pi\sum_i\|p_i-q_{\pi(i)}\|_2^2\). Its value here is0.5. The difference does not imply one is twice as accurate; the loss definitions have different normalization.

Balanced transport with nonuniform masses generalizes the assignment idea. It can penalize mass concentration that nearest-neighbor losses underemphasize, but its computation and meaning depend on the ground cost and mass constraints. Neither point metric alone certifies watertightness, topology, or absence of self-intersection. A dense point set on two disconnected pieces can be geometrically close to samples of a connected target while remaining unsuitable for fabrication.

Learn a surface map or learn a spatial query 51:22

AtlasNet learns parametric surface patches. A decoder \(s_\theta(u,v,z)\) takes patch coordinates and a shape code \(z\), and returns a3D point. Several patches can represent different parts of a complex surface. Sampling more parameter values increases the output sampling resolution, but cannot create detail the learned function does not contain. Patch overlaps, gaps, or poor parameterization remain possible.

Implicit networks instead take the spatial location as input. An occupancy network \(o_\theta(q,z)\in[0,1]\) predicts occupancy; a threshold defines a surface. A signed-distance network \(d_\theta(q,z)\in\mathbb R\) predicts distance with a chosen sign. The primary Occupancy Networks and DeepSDF papers establish these distinct query formulations. Binary cross-entropy fits occupancy labels; a distance regression objective fits signed-distance samples. They are different supervision signals.

Continuous query coordinates remove a fixed output-grid requirement, but not all resolution limits. Network capacity, coordinate encoding, training-sample coverage, and the final surface-extraction procedure constrain detail. If the training set rarely samples near a tiny recess, most loss terms may be satisfied while that recess is absent. Query sampling therefore plays the same role as class balancing did in our earlier classifier: it decides which errors training pays attention to.

A learned field also needs an object condition or per-scene parameters. Feeding only \(q\) to a single fixed network describes one learned field, not an arbitrary collection of independently selectable shapes. The conditioning code or separate network parameters identify which object is being queried.

Radiance fields connect3D queries to2D image losses 58:47

A radiance field returns volume density \(\sigma(q)\ge0\) and color \(c(q,d)\in\mathbb R^3\), where \(d\) is a viewing direction. In the original NeRF formulation, density depends on location and color also depends on direction. Density is not a signed distance or a dimensionless occupancy probability. For a ray \(r(t)=o+td\), with unit direction and metric distance parameter \(t\), density has inverse-length units.

Sample intervals of length \(\Delta_i\) along the ray. Define opacity \(\alpha_i=1-\exp(-\sigma_i\Delta_i)\), transmittance \(T_i=\prod_{j\lt i}(1-\alpha_j)\), and weight \(w_i=T_i\alpha_i\). The rendered color is \[ C=\sum_{i=1}^{K}w_i c_i+T_{K+1}c_{\mathrm{bg}}. \] Earlier intervals attenuate later contributions. Including background color \(c_{\mathrm{bg}}\) makes the remaining probability of transmission explicit. The weights plus the final transmittance sum to one; the object weights alone need not.

Our two-layer fixture uses opacities0.2 and0.5, red then blue, against black. Their weights are0.2 and0.4, with residual transmittance0.4, yielding \(C=(.2,0,.4)\). Reversing their depth order gives \(C=(.1,0,.5)\). Alpha compositing is not commutative. For scalar colors \(c_1=1,c_2=.25\), \(C=\alpha_1c_1+(1-\alpha_1)\alpha_2c_2\), so \(\partial C/\partial\alpha_1=c_1-\alpha_2c_2=.875\) and \(\partial C/\partial\alpha_2=(1-\alpha_1)c_2=.2\). The code checks these derivatives independently.

The primary NeRF paper fits a continuous radiance field using images and known camera poses through differentiable rendering. Image gradients can then update the field without direct dense3D shape supervision. The classical rendering model is an essential part of the system, not something the neural network must rediscover from scratch. The lecture also discusses generative radiance fields; pi-GAN combines a learned generative representation with volumetric rendering for3D-aware image synthesis.

Photometric agreement does not uniquely determine geometry, lighting, and appearance. Multiple views constrain ambiguity, but unseen regions remain unconstrained and view-dependent effects complicate interpretation. For our part, validate dimensions against independent depth or calibrated measurements instead of treating a low rendered-image loss as proof of geometric accuracy.

Gaussian splatting changes the representation and renderer 1:03:37

Querying many points along many rays can be expensive. The lecture motivates Gaussian splats as spatially localized primitives. The original 3D Gaussian Splatting paper optimizes explicit Gaussian positions, anisotropic covariances, opacities, and appearance coefficients. It projects and rasterizes these primitives with ordered alpha blending. It does not require a NeRF-style neural-network query at every ray sample; this corrects a simplification in the spoken account.

For a Gaussian with mean \(\mu\) and positive-definite covariance \(\Sigma\), a spatial profile is proportional to \(\exp[-\tfrac12(q-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(q-\mu)]\). Unequal covariance eigenvalues allow an elongated or flattened primitive. Such primitives can align with surfaces more efficiently than isotropic blobs. Their tails are mathematically nonzero everywhere, so practical localization includes truncation or culling choices.

The primary method's speed follows from representation, density control, and a specialized rasterizer together. The lecture's benchmark comparisons are historical results for particular scenes and implementations, not a universal speed ratio against every NeRF variant. Fast rendering also does not automatically provide clean mesh topology, collision geometry, or correct extrapolation outside the captured views. Those remain separate deliverables for our inspection and assembly system.

A part graph captures constraints that a surface alone omits 1:07:25

A surface representation can encode every bump while leaving the fact “these four supports should be symmetric” implicit. The lecture closes with part sets, relationship graphs, hierarchies, hierarchical graphs, and programs. A part set identifies components. Edges can encode adjacency or symmetry. A hierarchy expresses containment or decomposition. A hierarchical graph combines parent–child structure with lateral relationships between siblings.

The primary StructureNet paper develops hierarchical graph representations for structured shape generation. In our original factory example, a housing contains a shell and four mounting bosses; the bosses occupy related positions and must connect to the shell. A model that generates four visually plausible bosses independently can still leave one floating or misaligned. Relational constraints address a different failure than increasing surface resolution.

A procedural representation can generate repeated holes with a loop and a small number of dimensions. An LLM may propose such a program, as the lecture suggests, but semantic plausibility is not a guarantee of valid geometry. Execute the program in a constrained modeling environment, check dimensions and connectivity, and distinguish generated hypotheses from measured reconstruction. The representation should make the constraints needed by the next operation explicit.

Check set symmetry, geometry losses, and rendering gradients

This original NumPy experiment verifies local mechanisms. It does not reconstruct a scene or reproduce a paper benchmark.

import numpy as np
from itertools import permutations
from numerical_lab import central_gradient

points = np.array([[0., 1., 0.], [2., 0., 1.], [1., 2., 3.]])
W = np.array([[1., -1.], [2., 0.], [0., 1.]])
embed = lambda p: np.maximum(p @ W, 0).max(axis=0)
assert np.allclose(embed(points), embed(points[[2, 0, 1]]))
assert np.allclose(embed(points), embed(np.vstack([points, points[0]])))

P, Q = np.array([[0.], [2.]]), np.array([[0.], [3.]])
dist2 = ((P[:, None, :]-Q[None, :, :])**2).sum(axis=-1)
chamfer = dist2.min(axis=1).mean()+dist2.min(axis=0).mean()
matching = min(sum(dist2[i, j] for i, j in enumerate(perm))/2
               for perm in permutations(range(2)))
assert np.isclose(chamfer, 1.0) and np.isclose(matching, 0.5)
radii = np.array([0., 1.5, 2.5])
shell = np.maximum(radii-2, 1-radii)
assert np.array_equal(shell < 0, [False, True, False])

alpha = np.array([0.2, 0.5])
def render_scalar(a):
    return a[0]+(1-a[0])*a[1]*0.25
gradient = central_gradient(render_scalar, alpha)
assert np.allclose(gradient, [0.875, 0.2], atol=1e-8)
weights = np.array([alpha[0], (1-alpha[0])*alpha[1]])
transmission = np.prod(1-alpha)
assert np.isclose(weights.sum()+transmission, 1)
assert 256**3*4 == 64*1024**2
print("Chamfer, assignment, rendering gradient:", chamfer, matching, gradient)

Six exercises with complete solutions

1. Is the sphere's algebraic field a distance?

At \(q=(2,0,0)\), \(\|q\|^2-1=3\), while the nearest surface point is \((1,0,0)\), distance1 away. Both functions define the same sphere and signs, but only \(\|q\|-1\) is its signed distance. Scaling a field by a positive constant also preserves its zero set while changing all claimed distances.

2. Derive the eightfold dense-grid memory increase.

A grid with \(n\) positions per axis has \(n^3\) positions. Replacing \(n\) by \(2n\) yields \(8n^3\). For32 float32 channels at512 resolution, memory is \(512^3\cdot32\cdot4=17{,}179{,}869{,}184\)bytes, or16GiB. This excludes optimizer states and saved activations, so it is already a lower component of the training budget.

3. Why does symmetry not imply robustness to missing points?

Consider scalar features \(\{1,2,10\}\) pooled by maximum. Every permutation returns10, and duplicating1 changes nothing. Removing the point with feature10 returns2. Permutation and duplicate invariance are exact properties of max pooling; arbitrary resampling invariance is not. A missing small geometric feature can therefore disappear from the representation.

4. Compute the two point-cloud losses after a rigid translation.

Translate both \(P=\{0,2\}\) and \(Q=\{0,3\}\) by5. Pairwise differences do not change because \((p+5)-(q+5)=p-q\). Thus Chamfer remains1 and mean squared matching remains0.5. Translating only one cloud generally changes both losses, which is why registration must be specified before comparing shapes.

5. Show that compositing weights plus transmission sum to one.

Since \(T_{i+1}=T_i(1-\alpha_i)\), we have \(T_i\alpha_i=T_i-T_{i+1}\). Summing from1 to \(K\) telescopes to \(T_1-T_{K+1}=1-T_{K+1}\). Adding the background transmission gives1. This identity assumes the same ordered intervals are used throughout and \(T_1=1\).

6. Which representation should certify a mounting hole?

A radiance field or Gaussian scene can support visual inspection and novel views, but visible realism does not establish a clear passage. A calibrated distance or occupancy field can answer material queries along the passage; a valid mesh can support intersection and dimension checks. Independent measurements should validate the reconstruction, particularly where cameras cannot see. A part graph can additionally state the intended hole relation, but an intended relation is not evidence that the manufactured geometry satisfies it.
