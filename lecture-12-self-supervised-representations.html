Lecture 12 · Learning from unlabelled observations

Self-supervision: choose what a representation must preserve

Removing manual labels does not remove supervision. It moves the design decision into the relationships, transformations, and missing information used to construct a learning task.

Our inspection system has millions of factory images but only a small number of carefully labelled defects. A representation learned from those few labels may overfit to one camera or production line. Self-supervised learning offers a different starting point: train an encoder on automatically constructed tasks, then adapt its features to the labelled application. The central question is not whether an image reconstruction looks attractive. It is whether the training signal preserves the distinctions that inspection needs.

This chapter follows the lecture's progression through historical pretext tasks, masked autoencoders, and contrastive learning. The short discussion of predictive coding and DINO at the end distinguishes the lecture's brief mention from the fuller slide and primary-paper extension. All numerical fixtures are original teaching examples; they are not reproduced benchmark results.

A useful feature is defined by its downstream use 00:05

An encoder \(f_\theta\) maps an image \(x\) to a feature vector \(h=f_\theta(x)\in\mathbb R^d\), where \(\theta\) denotes learned parameters. “Feature,” “embedding,” and “representation” refer here to that output, not to different mathematical species. A downstream predictor \(a_\psi(h)\) uses the representation for classification, segmentation, retrieval, or another task. The choice of which layer supplies \(h\) changes the representation being evaluated.

In supervised training, labels provide a direct target. In self-supervised pretraining, we construct an input \(u=T(x)\) and a target \(v=R(x)\) from available observations. A generic objective is

\[\min_{\theta,\phi}\mathbb E_{x,T,R}\big[\ell(g_\phi(f_\theta(T(x))),R(x))\big].\]

The function \(g_\phi\) is a task-specific head or decoder, and \(\ell\) compares its prediction with the constructed target. The target comes from the data or transformation procedure; the decoder does not magically provide an independent ground truth. Gradients through \(g_\phi\) train the encoder because the encoder's output affects the loss.

After pretraining, we can discard the head and train a different predictor on labelled examples. An encoder and decoder need not have matching architectures or sizes. They are modules selected to make the pretext objective informative and tractable. “Unsupervised” is often used broadly for this family because human labels are absent, but the optimization still uses explicit targets and losses.

For factory inspection, hiding a region and predicting it could teach shape regularities. Predicting the camera identifier could also be solved without manual labels, yet encourage precisely the nuisance dependence we wanted to remove. Automatic target availability is therefore necessary for this workflow, but insufficient for a good representation.

Separate pretext success from transferable information 10:30

A linear probe freezes \(f_\theta\) and learns a linear classifier \(Wh+b\) on labelled training data. It tests whether the chosen label is accessible through a restricted readout. Fine-tuning also updates some or all encoder parameters. It measures adaptation under a specified training budget. Neither protocol is automatically superior: they answer different questions, and a weak linear probe does not prove that fine-tuning cannot succeed.

Keep the labelled split, architecture, augmentation, optimization budget, and selection rule explicit. A model that saw a test image during unlabelled pretraining has a different exposure history from one that did not. In our case, splitting adjacent video frames randomly can leak almost identical parts across train and test sets. Split by production run or physical item before constructing views, then report which factories and cameras are held out.

Nearest neighbors and clustering reveal geometry, while two-dimensional visualizations such as t-SNE can suggest patterns. A visually separated plot does not establish calibrated classification or out-of-distribution robustness. A projection can distort distances and hide overlap. Downstream error costs, performance by lighting condition, and sample efficiency with limited labels are more directly connected to inspection.

Imagine representation A predicts rotation perfectly by recognizing a camera watermark, while representation B predicts it less well but groups part shapes consistently across cameras. A can win the pretext metric and lose defect detection. This is a shortcut: the task admits a solution that bypasses the intended semantic structure. Good evaluation must be able to expose that failure rather than assume that pretext accuracy certifies understanding.

Predict transformations without inviting shortcuts 15:43

Rotation prediction trains a classifier to distinguish rotations such as \(0^\circ,90^\circ,180^\circ,270^\circ\). If \(r\in\{0,1,2,3\}\) is the selected quarter-turn and \(p_\theta(r\mid T_r x)\) its predicted probability, the loss is \(-\log p_\theta(r\mid T_r x)\). The hypothesis is that recognizing an object's usual orientation encourages meaningful features. It is a hypothesis about the data, not a theorem that rotation accuracy implies object recognition.

A perfectly symmetric washer provides a counterexample. Its four rotated appearances can be identical, so no visual classifier can recover the randomly selected rotation better than chance from that image alone. Under uniform rotations, an optimal prediction is \(1/4\) for each class and the irreducible cross-entropy is \(\log4\). Conversely, interpolation artifacts or fixed text borders can reveal a rotation without revealing the part. Quarter-turns on a square pixel grid help avoid some interpolation artifacts but do not remove all shortcuts.

The lecture next considers relative patch position. Given a center patch and another patch, predict one of eight neighboring positions. A joint image structure can make this solvable. Yet matching a continuous edge across patch boundaries can bypass broad object understanding. Spacing, jitter, and other preprocessing choices determine which cues remain available.

Jigsaw prediction generalizes the idea to rearranged patches. Nine distinct patches have \(9!=362,880\) permutations. A finite selected set turns the problem into manageable classification. The lecture illustrates a 64-class subset; that is a design example, not a mathematical requirement or a claim that every experiment in the original paper used 64 classes. Selecting permutations with substantial differences reduces nearly identical classification cases.

Inspection sometimes needs orientation rather than invariance to it. A connector inserted backwards is defective precisely because its arrangement differs. Rotation prediction encourages orientation-sensitive features, whereas forcing all rotations to have identical embeddings can erase that signal. The downstream task determines whether a transformation should be predicted, ignored, or represented equivariantly. Equivariance means the representation changes in a predictable way when the input changes; invariance means it does not change.

Missing pixels create targets and ambiguity 26:33

Inpainting removes part of an image and predicts the missing values from context. Let \(x\in\mathbb R^P\) contain \(P\) scalar pixel-channel entries and \(m\in\{0,1\}^P\) mark hidden entries with one. The corrupted input is \((1-m)\odot x\), where \(\odot\) denotes entrywise multiplication. For reconstruction \(\hat x\), a normalized masked squared loss is

\[L_{\mathrm{rec}}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{P}m_j(\hat x_j-x_j)^2}{\sum_{j=1}^{P}m_j},\qquad \frac{\partial L_{\mathrm{rec}}}{\partial\hat x_j} =\frac{2m_j(\hat x_j-x_j)}{\sum_km_k}.\]

The denominator requires at least one masked entry. It prevents a larger hidden region from automatically giving a larger loss solely because it has more entries. A different normalization is possible, but must be stated. Visible entries receive zero direct reconstruction derivative under this objective, although their encoded features still influence hidden predictions and receive gradients through that path.

Consider \(x=(1,2,3,4)\), prediction \(\hat x=(9,1,8,6)\), and mask \(m=(0,1,0,1)\). Only errors \(-1\) and \(2\) count, giving loss \(2.5\) and output gradient \((0,-1,0,2)\). Penalizing the visible entries would produce a different objective and could encourage copying rather than contextual inference.

Why do squared-error reconstructions blur? Given visible context \(c\), minimize \(\mathbb E[(a-X)^2\mid c]\) over a scalar prediction \(a\). Differentiation gives \(2(a-\mathbb E[X\mid c])=0\), so the optimum is the conditional mean. If a hidden feature is equally likely to be \(-1\) or \(1\), the mean is zero even though zero is never an actual outcome. Pixelwise averaging of several plausible structures can look unrealistic.

Context Encoders combine reconstruction with an adversarial objective to improve plausible completion. Plausibility does not identify the historically correct hidden content. In inspection, a model that fills a crack with smooth metal may produce a convincing image while destroying evidence. The next chapter develops generative objectives; here the distinction already tells us why a decoder's visual quality and an encoder's diagnostic usefulness need separate tests.

Cross-channel prediction learns shared structure 30:52

Colorization uses channels that already exist in the data. In the CIELAB representation, \(L^*\) describes lightness and \(a^*,b^*\) describe chromatic components. Predicting chromatic channels from lightness requires correlations between appearance and color. It does not mean that lightness uniquely determines color. Several paint colors may have similar luminance.

Colorful Image Colorization models uncertainty using color classification and rebalancing rather than treating the task as universally deterministic regression. The choice of loss changes what the decoder expresses. A distribution over plausible colors can preserve alternatives that a single mean suppresses. Colorizing an archival image consequently produces a plausible interpretation, not evidence of its original colors.

Split-Brain Autoencoders train disjoint subnetworks in both directions between channel groups. If \(x=(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\), one network predicts \(x^{(2)}\) from \(x^{(1)}\), while the other predicts \(x^{(1)}\) from \(x^{(2)}\). Their internal features can be concatenated for transfer. The idea also applies to RGB and depth, but sensor alignment and missing depth values then affect the targets.

For our inspection data, chromatic staining may be decisive while part geometry is nearly constant. A colorization pretext task could help connect texture with material, yet aggressively discarding color during later augmentation could undo that benefit. Conversely, dependence on lighting color can be a nuisance. We must distinguish physically meaningful color from camera white balance through the evaluation protocol, not through the name of the pretraining method.

Color propagation makes correspondence useful 37:29

Video provides another automatically available relation: the same surface often persists over nearby frames. Tracking Emerges by Colorizing Videos predicts colors by copying from a reference frame through learned correspondences. Let \(q_i\) be a target pixel's feature, \(k_j\) a reference feature, and \(c_j\) its color vector. A normalized similarity gives

\[a_{ij}=\frac{\exp(q_i^\top k_j/\tau)}{\sum_r\exp(q_i^\top k_r/\tau)},\qquad \hat c_i=\sum_j a_{ij}c_j,\quad \tau\gt0.\]

This is an attention operation with color values. The temperature \(\tau\) controls score concentration. To copy the right color, the system benefits from matching corresponding regions; the learned weights can then propagate masks or keypoints. That connection motivates the tracking hypothesis, rather than proving every color match is a valid physical trajectory.

For two reference colors \(c_1=(1,0)\) and \(c_2=(0,1)\), weights \(0.75,0.25\) produce \(\hat c=(0.75,0.25)\). Two unrelated surfaces with the same color are indistinguishable to a color-only loss. Occlusion can also remove the correct source from the reference frame. Thus a low colorization error can coexist with an incorrect correspondence, and uncertainty should not be mistaken for exact tracking.

Our moving conveyor illustrates both sides. Color propagation can link a part across frames without hand-labelled tracks. Repeated identical parts create distractors, however, so time spacing, spatial context, and evaluation on actual identity changes matter. A target that rewards copying is only as informative as the correspondence constraints it creates.

Mask aggressively while encoding only visible patches 45:52

A masked autoencoder, or MAE, combines a large vision-transformer encoder with a lighter reconstruction decoder. Split an image into \(N\) patches and mask a fraction \(\rho\). Only the \(N_v=(1-\rho)N\) visible patches enter the encoder. Patch projections retain their original positional information. After encoding, their representations are inserted into the full patch layout together with a shared learned mask token for missing positions. The decoder reconstructs pixels, and the loss is applied to hidden patches.

Why can one shared mask token serve many positions? It communicates missingness; positional embeddings distinguish the locations. It is a trainable vector, not a guaranteed average image patch. Without positional information, exchanging two identical mask tokens would not tell the decoder which missing location was intended. Restoring the original ordering is therefore part of the algorithm, not bookkeeping that can be ignored.

For a \(224\times224\) image and \(16\times16\) patches, \(N=14^2=196\). At \(\rho=0.75\), the encoder receives 49 patches and the decoder reconstructs the full layout containing 147 hidden patches. Ignoring an optional class token, an encoder attention matrix has \(49^2=2401\) entries instead of \(196^2=38,416\), a sixteenfold reduction in that matrix's size. Feedforward costs scale linearly in token count, while decoder and projection costs remain. The whole model is therefore not automatically sixteen times faster.

Random masks can make reconstruction depend on widely separated visible context. A very easy mask can be solved by local interpolation; an almost completely hidden image can become uninformative. The lecture highlights 75% as an effective empirical setting for the studied MAE models, not a universal optimum. Different patch sizes, textures, modalities, and objectives change the useful ratio.

Rare defects expose a tradeoff. A mask may hide the only evidence of a crack, and ordinary image statistics may favor reconstructing an intact surface. Nevertheless, a pretrained encoder may learn valuable material and geometric features. Evaluate transfer on small labelled defect sets and inspect failures by defect size. “Harder pretext task” does not monotonically mean “better representation”; an impossible or irrelevant task can spend computation learning little that helps.

Contrastive learning is classification among candidates 58:41

Contrastive learning constructs a positive relation and competing candidates. An anchor \(u\) might be one view of a part; its positive \(v_+\) is another augmentation of the same source image. Negatives are other sampled views. Their identities are known from the construction, even though object class labels are unknown. “Different image” is not synonymous with “different semantic class,” an important limitation when many parts look alike.

Let \(s_j=s(u,v_j)\) be a scalar similarity and \(z_j=s_j/\tau\) a temperature-scaled logit. Among \(K\) candidates, one indexed \(+\) is positive. The InfoNCE-style loss is

\[p_j=\frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_{r=1}^{K}e^{z_r}},\qquad L=-\log p_+=-\frac{s_+}{\tau}+\log\sum_{r=1}^{K}e^{s_r/\tau},\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial s_j}=\frac{p_j-\mathbf1[j=+]}{\tau}.\]

The positive belongs in the denominator. The derivative increases its score under gradient descent and decreases competing scores, weighted by their predicted probability. Hard negatives receive larger gradients than already implausible ones. When similarities are cosine values, normalize nonzero feature vectors and define how near-zero norms are handled. Temperature rescales both concentration and gradients.

Our fixture uses logits \((\log4,\log2,\log1)\), with the first candidate positive. The probabilities are \((4/7,2/7,1/7)\); loss is \(\log(7/4)\approx0.5596\). The derivative with respect to logits is \((-3/7,2/7,1/7)\). These values provide a small test whose correct answer can be derived independently of a library implementation.

The CPC paper connects this loss with mutual information. Under its joint-positive and independently sampled marginal-negative construction, the expected loss yields \(I(U;V)\geq\log K-\mathbb E[L]\). The constant \(\log K\) matters. A low loss maximizes a lower bound under the assumptions; it does not directly measure semantic usefulness. The bound cannot exceed \(\log K\), and arbitrary hard-negative sampling or dependent candidates need separate analysis.

More candidates can increase the information bound's capacity, but “larger batch always improves learning” is not a theorem. False negatives, optimization, data diversity, and compute budget interact. For identical manufactured parts, aggressively repelling all other instances may separate incidental scratches, lighting, or camera noise rather than the defect category we care about.

SimCLR separates the readout space from the contrastive space 66:04

SimCLR draws two random augmentations of each of \(N\) source images. The encoder produces \(h\), a projection head produces \(z=g(h)\), and the contrastive objective uses normalized projected features. The original method emphasizes a nonlinear projection head; treating its successful head as necessarily linear misses an important empirical finding.

There are \(2N\) views. For each anchor, exclude its self-comparison, leaving one positive and \(2N-2\) negatives, or \(2N-1\) denominator terms. Average the loss over all anchors, so each positive pair is used in both directions. A diagonal self-similarity is usually extremely large and supplies no useful discrimination; accidentally retaining it makes the classification problem different.

The projection head allows the contrastive space to discard information unnecessary for view matching while the encoder representation remains useful for transfer. It does not guarantee that all discarded information survives in \(h\). This is an architectural opportunity tested empirically. A downstream probe commonly uses \(h\), so record which representation was evaluated.

Augmentations define the desired agreement. Random crops, color changes, and blur work well in many natural-image settings. For inspection, a crop that removes the defect should not automatically be treated as an equivalent diagnostic view. One useful experiment compares unconstrained random crops against crops that preserve a physically meaningful region, evaluating both on held-out defect localization. The comparison tests a concrete invariance assumption rather than treating augmentation as harmless noise.

Distributed training adds another choice: whether candidates include other devices' views and whether gradients through gathered features are retained correctly. Large candidate sets consume memory, and gathering features is not the same as averaging parameter gradients. The preceding chapter's discipline applies: specify the loss, then verify that the distributed computation matches it.

MoCo enlarges the dictionary without enlarging every batch 71:18

Momentum Contrast, or MoCo, maintains a queue of encoded keys from earlier batches. A query encoder learns by gradient descent; a key encoder changes by an exponential moving average of query parameters. If \(\theta_q\) and \(\theta_k\) denote their parameters, the update is

\[\theta_k\leftarrow m\theta_k+(1-m)\theta_q,\qquad 0\leq m\lt1.\]

The queue decouples dictionary size from the current batch. Keys are treated as fixed targets for the current contrastive update, including the current positive key; the key encoder is not merely an encoder of negative examples. Slow parameter movement makes keys computed at different times more consistent. It does not make them identical to encodings under the newest model.

For \(m=0.9\), old key parameter 2 and current query parameter 4 yield a new key value 2.2. After repeated updates, the influence of an old key-encoder state decays geometrically as \(m^t\). This is a time-scale choice, not the momentum of the optimizer applied directly to a loss gradient. A queue of capacity \(Q\), with \(b\) new keys inserted per step, spans roughly \(Q/b\) recent steps once full.

The lecture discusses MoCo v2 as combining queue-based training with strong augmentation and a nonlinear head. Its comparisons belong to specific published settings; no architecture is guaranteed to dominate every dataset. Our repeated-part dataset also demands care about false negatives in the queue and train/test separation when constructing it.

Prediction and self-distillation broaden the target 73:45

The spoken lecture closes with brief pointers. The slides give more detail on CPC: encode a sequence as \(z_t=f(x_t)\), summarize past observations into context \(c_t\), and distinguish the actual future code from alternatives using a score such as \(z_{t+k}^{\top}W_kc_t\). The matrix \(W_k\) is specific to prediction horizon \(k\). A causal context must not accidentally include the target. This predicts useful latent information rather than requiring every future pixel to be generated.

DINO uses student and momentum-teacher predictions across views, with cross-entropy between output distributions rather than an explicit dictionary of negative instances. Teacher targets are stopped from receiving the student's gradient. Centering and sharpening, together with the momentum teacher and view design, help avoid collapsed representations. Agreement alone would admit a useless solution in which every image produces the same output.

These methods show that learning without manual class labels need not mean one particular reconstruction or contrastive loss. The slide appendix also points toward dense descriptors for object correspondence. The unifying question is which relations the target forces features to preserve. Our inspection project may need local correspondence, rare defect sensitivity, and temporal persistence at once; success on one relation does not establish the others.

Check a masked loss and a contrastive derivative

The following original NumPy code checks the exact fixtures and uses central finite differences to verify the logit derivative. It also constructs a two-image, four-view contrastive batch whose symmetry gives a closed-form loss. It trains no encoder; its purpose is to catch loss-definition errors before representation experiments begin.

import numpy as np
from numerical_lab import central_gradient

x = np.array([1., 2., 3., 4.])
prediction = np.array([9., 1., 8., 6.])
mask = np.array([0., 1., 0., 1.])
masked_loss = np.sum(mask * (prediction - x)**2) / mask.sum()
masked_grad = 2 * mask * (prediction - x) / mask.sum()
assert np.isclose(masked_loss, 2.5)
assert np.allclose(masked_grad, [0., -1., 0., 2.])

def nce(logits):
    shifted = logits - np.max(logits)
    return -shifted[0] + np.log(np.exp(shifted).sum())
logits = np.log([4., 2., 1.])
p = np.exp(logits - np.max(logits))
p /= p.sum()
analytic = p - np.array([1., 0., 0.])
assert np.isclose(nce(logits), np.log(7 / 4))
assert np.allclose(analytic, [-3/7, 2/7, 1/7])
assert np.allclose(central_gradient(nce, logits), analytic, atol=1e-8)

# Two identical views of each of two opposite unit vectors.
views = np.array([[1., 0.], [1., 0.], [-1., 0.], [-1., 0.]])
partners = [1, 0, 3, 2]
scores = views @ views.T
losses = []
for i, partner in enumerate(partners):
    candidates = np.delete(np.arange(4), i)
    row = scores[i, candidates]
    positive = np.flatnonzero(candidates == partner)[0]
    losses.append(-row[positive] + np.log(np.exp(row).sum()))
assert np.allclose(losses, np.log(1 + 2 * np.exp(-2)))
assert 196 * 0.25 == 49
assert np.isclose(0.9 * 2 + 0.1 * 4, 2.2)
print("Masked reconstruction, InfoNCE gradient, and view indexing passed.")

Six problems with worked solutions

1. Rotation information that does not exist

A circular, textureless part has exactly the same image after every quarter-turn. Rotations are selected uniformly. What is the smallest expected four-class log loss?

Solution. The input conveys no information about the selected class, so the conditional distribution is uniform. Predicting \(1/4\) minimizes expected cross-entropy, giving \(-\log(1/4)=\log4\approx1.3863\). Better-than-chance performance suggests an unintended cue or a violation of the symmetry assumption.

2. Why a reconstruction can be implausible

Given visible context, a missing scalar is \(-2\) with probability \(1/4\) and \(2\) with probability \(3/4\). Find its minimum-MSE prediction and expected error.

Solution. The conditional mean is \(a=1\). Its error is \((1/4)(1+2)^2+(3/4)(1-2)^2=3\). Predicting the more likely actual value 2 gives expected error 4. MSE therefore prefers a value that never occurs in this two-point distribution.

3. The masked-autoencoder compute claim

An image has 256 patches. Mask 75%. How many visible patches and encoder attention entries remain? Does that determine total speedup?

Solution. There are 64 visible patches, so the attention matrix has \(64^2=4096\) entries instead of \(256^2=65,536\), a sixteenfold reduction. Feedforward work falls roughly fourfold in token count, while decoding and other operations remain. Total speedup requires an actual cost model and measurement.

4. Temperature is part of the gradient

For similarities \(s=(\log4,\log2,\log1)\) and temperature 1, compute the derivative for a positive first entry. What expression changes at arbitrary temperature?

Solution. The probabilities are \(4/7,2/7,1/7\), and the derivative is \((-3/7,2/7,1/7)\). At arbitrary \(\tau\), recompute \(p=\operatorname{softmax}(s/\tau)\), then divide \(p-e_1\) by \(\tau\). Merely dividing the temperature-1 derivative is wrong because the probabilities also change.

5. Count the SimCLR candidates

A batch contains 32 source images, each with two views. For each anchor, how many positives, negatives, and denominator terms are there?

Solution. There are 64 views. Excluding the anchor itself leaves 63 candidates: one positive and 62 negatives. Averaging over 64 anchors uses each positive relationship in both directions. If several source images show the same physical part, the nominal negatives may be semantically related despite correct indexing.

6. A queue and an information bound

A MoCo queue holds 4096 keys and receives 256 per step. Separately, an idealized InfoNCE experiment has \(K=128\) candidates and expected loss 2 nats. State the queue time span and the information bound, with its limitation.

Solution. The full queue spans about \(4096/256=16\) recent steps. Under the joint-positive and marginal-negative assumptions, the bound is \(\log128-2\approx2.852\) nats. It is a lower bound on mutual information for the variables and sampling scheme, not a defect-recognition score. A finite stale queue is not automatically an independent sample satisfying every assumption of that idealized derivation.

The target determines what can be learned

Self-supervision converts abundant observations into prediction problems. Rotation and arrangement expose geometry; reconstruction and colorization expose conditional structure; video propagation rewards correspondence; contrastive objectives reward selected agreements and distinctions. MAE changes which tokens receive expensive encoding, while MoCo changes how candidate keys are maintained. These are different interventions in the learning problem.
