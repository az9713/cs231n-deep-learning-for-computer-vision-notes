Chapter 18 · CS231N, Spring 2025

Human-centered vision: capability, restraint, and useful action

A vision system can recognize an activity accurately and still be the wrong system to deploy. Human-centered vision asks which task matters, who bears each error, what the sensor reveals, and whether the resulting action actually helps.

Grounded in the complete final lecture by Fei-Fei Li. The three-part sequence—seeing what humans see, what humans do not see, and what humans want to see—follows the recording. The hospital-room case, formal decision model, calculations, code, and exercises are original teaching constructions. They are not reported clinical results.

1. A monitor can observe more than its task requires 00:05

An older patient is recovering in a hospital room. Clinicians want to know whether the patient leaves the bed without assistance, because an unnoticed attempt can end in a fall. A conventional video camera contains useful evidence about posture and movement. It also records faces, visitors, screens, possessions, and intimate care. The engineering question is therefore not simply whether a neural network can classify “leaving bed.” It is whether a sensing-and-response system can produce a clinically useful intervention while limiting unrelated observation and avoiding new failure modes.

Let \(x\) denote the physical scene over a short time interval. A sensor \(S\) produces a representation \(z=S(x)\), such as RGB frames, depth silhouettes, or a deliberately coded optical measurement. A task model \(f\) produces a score \(r=f(z)\), and a decision rule \(d_\tau\) raises an alert when \(r\ge \tau\), where \(\tau\) is a chosen threshold. The true task label \(y\in\{0,1\}\) records whether assistance was actually needed. A separate sensitive attribute \(a\), such as identity, may also be inferable from \(z\), even though it is irrelevant to the task.

This decomposition exposes four design choices: what the sensor retains, what the model predicts, how the threshold converts a score into an action, and what staff do after an alert. Accuracy of \(f\) measures only part of the chain. A perfect classifier that pages nobody has no clinical benefit. An oversensitive system can create alarm fatigue. A raw-video archive can create privacy and security risks even if the classifier never uses identity.

Define the true-positive rate \(s=P(d_\tau=1\mid y=1)\), the false-positive rate \(q=P(d_\tau=1\mid y=0)\), and prevalence \(\pi=P(y=1)\). If one missed event costs \(c_{\rm FN}\) decision units and one false alert costs \(c_{\rm FP}\), expected decision loss per monitored interval is

\[ L(\tau)=\pi(1-s)c_{\rm FN}+(1-\pi)q c_{\rm FP}. \]

Suppose \(\pi=0.02\), \(s=0.90\), \(q=0.05\), \(c_{\rm FN}=100\), and \(c_{\rm FP}=1\). The missed-event term is \(0.02(0.10)(100)=0.20\); the false-alert term is \(0.98(0.05)=0.049\), for total loss \(0.249\). The large miss cost makes sensitivity important, but the low prevalence means even a modest false-positive rate produces many alerts. No universal threshold follows: the costs, prevalence, staffing process, and consequences must be estimated for the intended setting.

A human-centered objective cannot hide privacy inside an unspecified penalty. Let \(A\) be a declared adversary trained to infer identity from \(z\), and let \(R_{\rm priv}(S,A)\) be its test accuracy under a fixed protocol. A defensible design can state a constrained problem: minimize task loss subject to measured identity inference no better than a stated bound and subject to an alert-volume limit. Passing that test does not prove “privacy”; a different adversary or auxiliary dataset may extract other information. It does turn a slogan into a falsifiable threat model.

2. Seeing what humans see required data, models, and sharper tasks 04:24

The lecture first reviews the field’s attempt to reproduce human visual capability. People recognize objects rapidly despite changes in viewpoint, illumination, occlusion, background, and scale. A digital image does not label which variations are irrelevant. A recognition system must learn or encode a representation that preserves category evidence while tolerating specified nuisance variation.

Early compositional approaches represented an object through designed parts and spatial relations. Statistical learning shifted some parameter choices from the designer to data. Large datasets then changed which model families could be tested. The original ImageNet paper describes a large, hierarchically organized image database built around WordNet concepts. The database, benchmark protocol, neural architectures, optimization, and computing hardware were distinct contributions. Treating “deep learning” as one isolated algorithm erases the experimental system that made progress measurable.

Object labels are not a complete account of a scene. A person beside a llama and a person riding a llama contain similar object sets but different relations. A scene graph represents entities as nodes and relations as labeled edges. The Visual Genome paper connects dense region descriptions, objects, attributes, and relationships in one dataset. A graph can express “person-holds-cup”; it does not automatically determine whether the action is safe, consensual, or relevant to the user’s question.

Compositional representations can support unusual combinations. If a model has learned the concepts horse, hat, and wearing, it may score “horse wearing hat” without many examples of that exact triple. Lu and colleagues’ visual relationship detection work uses language priors to address long-tailed relations. The general lesson is conditional: factorization can share evidence across combinations, but only when the learned factors and their composition remain valid. A scene graph with a wrong entity or relation gives structured error rather than structured truth.

Captioning and video understanding enlarge the output again. A caption selects some events and omits others; a dynamic-scene model must associate actors, actions, temporal order, and camera motion. Evaluation becomes harder because several descriptions may be valid and because a fluent sentence can contain a consequential hallucination. The correct benchmark unit may be an episode or interaction, rather than an independently scored frame.

For the hospital monitor, “person,” “bed,” and “standing” are intermediate concepts. The clinical target depends on relations and time: the patient moves from supported lying to unsupported standing while no helper is present. A model that recognizes every object but loses temporal order cannot answer the task. Each expansion from object to relation to activity changes the annotation, metric, and error analysis.

3. Seeing what humans do not see changes the unit of inference 23:29

The second lecture movement considers scale and regularity beyond unaided human attention. Fine-grained recognition distinguishes visually similar bird species or vehicle variants. Population-scale vision can summarize millions of street images. Continuous sensing can notice events that a periodically present human observer misses. These capabilities are not one-dimensional “superhuman vision”; each exceeds a particular human budget under a particular protocol.

Gebru and colleagues analyzed vehicles in 50 million Google Street View images across 200 US cities. Their PNAS study used detected vehicle distributions to estimate regional socioeconomic attributes and voting patterns. The unit of inference was an aggregate geographic region. A correlation between vehicle mixtures and a precinct-level outcome does not license inferring the politics, race, or income of an individual car owner. That leap is an ecological fallacy.

Write \(C\) for a region’s vector of vehicle-category proportions and \(G\) for a regional statistic. A fitted predictor \(\widehat G=g(C)\) estimates an association in the sampled regions. Even if its held-out error is small, it does not establish a causal effect of cars on \(G\), nor a conditional probability for a particular resident. Sampling coverage, image age, detector error, geographic splits, and the definition of \(G\) all affect the conclusion.

Human attention also has limits. Change blindness and the Stroop effect show that perception is selective and task-dependent. A continuous monitor can apply the same rule to every interval, but consistency is not correctness. If the rule encodes a systematic blind spot, automation repeats it at scale. A hospital study must therefore compare against a defined reference process, report uncertain labels and disagreements, and test whether the intervention improves patient outcomes rather than merely increasing event counts.

The lecture describes ambient intelligence as sensors and models that extract health-relevant information from care environments. Haque and colleagues’ Nature perspective surveys uses such as ICU mobilization, surgical workflow, hand hygiene, and home monitoring while emphasizing privacy, bias, security, and clinical validation. A research demonstration that recognizes an event is upstream of a safe clinical product. Integration requires prospective evaluation, staff workflow, failure escalation, maintenance, consent, and governance.

The hospital case makes denominators unavoidable. If 9 of 10 true events are detected, sensitivity is 90%; if the system also emits 49 false alarms among 980 negative intervals, specificity is 95%. Positive predictive value is only \(9/(9+49)\approx15.5\%\). Most alerts are false because the event is rare. Reporting “90% detection” without prevalence and false alerts leaves the operator unable to predict workload.

4. Privacy is a property of a system and threat model 32:32

Privacy cannot be reduced to whether an image looks blurry to a casual viewer. A representation may hide faces while preserving gait, body shape, room layout, or temporal patterns that identify a person. Conversely, destroying all visual structure protects many attributes but also destroys the activity signal. The design problem is to retain evidence required for the declared task while reducing attack surfaces under explicit assumptions.

Useful interventions can occur at several layers. The sensor may capture depth instead of color. Optics may encode incoming light before a conventional image exists. On-device processing may discard raw measurements after computing an event. Access control, retention limits, encryption, audit logs, and federated optimization address different threats. Federated learning changes where model updates are computed; without additional protections it does not guarantee that updates reveal no sensitive information.

Wang and colleagues’ coded-aperture study explores action recognition from measurements designed to be visually unintelligible, with preliminary simulated results and mixed prototype results. The paper’s limitations matter as much as its mechanism. “Hard for a person to view” and “non-invertible to a capable attacker with auxiliary data” are different claims.

Let \(U(S)\) be task performance of sensor design \(S\), and let \(P_j(S)\) be performance of privacy attacker \(j\) under a fixed train/test protocol. A simple constrained comparison is

\[ \max_{S\in\mathcal S_{\rm allowed}} U(S) \quad\text{subject to}\quad P_j(S)\le b_j\quad\text{for every tested attacker }j. \]

The set \(\mathcal S_{\rm allowed}\) records hardware, latency, and cost constraints; \(b_j\) is a predeclared leakage limit. Multiple attackers are necessary because suppressing face classification may leave re-identification by motion. This optimization still yields only empirical evidence against tested attacks. It should be accompanied by data minimization: if an intermediate representation need not be retained, deletion removes a future exposure that no benchmark accuracy captures.

Human values also differ. A patient may accept a depth silhouette for fall prevention but reject continuous audio; a clinician may need a short review window to assess an alert; a visitor may not have chosen either. Designing consent, notice, access, and appeal is part of the socio-technical system. Mathematical trade-offs organize evidence; they do not determine whose preferences govern a shared room.

5. Seeing what humans want begins with augmentation, not a capability demo 38:24

The lecture’s third movement connects perception to desired assistance. Automation changes labor, sometimes painfully, while many care settings simultaneously face shortages. “Replacement” and “augmentation” should be tested as operational hypotheses. Does the system reduce time spent searching for information? Does it create additional verification work? Does it shift difficult tasks to less visible workers? Does it improve outcomes for patients?

For the bedside monitor, the relevant intervention is not an alert on a dashboard. It is a timely response by a person with the authority and capacity to help. Let \(p_{\rm det}\) be event-detection probability, \(p_{\rm attend}\) the probability that staff attend a valid alert in time, and \(p_{\rm prevent}\) the probability that timely attendance prevents harm. Under a simplified conditional-independence chain, preventable-event coverage is

\[ C=p_{\rm det}\,p_{\rm attend}\,p_{\rm prevent}. \]

If the three probabilities are 0.90, 0.70, and 0.80, then \(C=0.504\). Improving detector sensitivity from 0.90 to 0.95 raises coverage only to 0.532 if the downstream factors stay fixed. The product model is an original teaching approximation; the factors may interact because false alarms reduce attendance and difficult events may be harder both to detect and prevent. It nevertheless shows why a model metric is not an outcome metric.

Human-centered evaluation should preserve a vector of outcomes before assigning weights: missed critical events, false alerts, response time, staff workload, privacy incidents, subgroup performance, patient experience, and clinical harm. An aggregate utility can support a decision only after stakeholders state how these outcomes are valued. Hiding them in one score makes disagreement look like arithmetic.

Subgroup evaluation needs sufficient denominators and uncertainty. Equal average loss can conceal a sensor that fails on mobility aids, blankets, skin tones, room layouts, or body types. Conversely, a noisy difference from five events is not a stable estimate. Report counts, confidence intervals or posterior uncertainty, and the data-generating conditions. A fairness metric selected after viewing results can become another form of benchmark overfitting.

6. Embodied systems close the loop and inherit its consequences 47:58

A sensor observes; a robot changes the world. Closing that loop makes representation errors consequential. An open instruction such as “open the top drawer without hitting the vase” requires language grounding, object localization, geometric constraints, motion planning, control, and feedback. A language model that emits plausible code supplies neither collision geometry nor a guarantee that a visual-language model located the correct vase.

Let \(g\) be a desired goal region in state space and \(\mathcal C_{\rm safe}\) a set of states satisfying collision and contact constraints. A planned trajectory \(x_{0:T}\) is acceptable only if \(x_T\in g\) and \(x_t\in\mathcal C_{\rm safe}\) for every time \(t\). A heat map suggesting where to move can contribute a cost term, but it does not by itself establish either condition. Execution also needs state estimation and feedback because the object may move differently from the model.

The lecture presents household benchmarks as a response to closed, short-horizon laboratory tasks. The original BEHAVIOR benchmark formalized 100 everyday activities with object-centered logical conditions and human demonstrations. BEHAVIOR-1K expands to 1,000 activities selected through a survey of desired robot assistance, 50 scenes, and more than 9,000 richly annotated objects. These benchmarks connect task selection to expressed human preferences and expose long-horizon physical complexity.

A simulator can repeat rare failures and provide privileged state for evaluation. It remains a model. A policy can exploit simulator-specific perception, contact, or reset behavior; success in simulation does not establish safe transfer. Conversely, a benchmark score of zero under a strict unprivileged setting identifies a gap between research assumptions and the intended task. Relaxing the task with perfect segmentation or teleportation can diagnose which capability is missing, provided the relaxed result is not reported as real-world autonomy.

Human preference does not collapse into majority vote. People may welcome floor cleaning yet reject robots handling sentimental objects; preferences can depend on disability, culture, household roles, cost, trust, and the particular robot. Survey sampling affects whose work is prioritized. A benchmark can make its task-selection process inspectable, but downstream designers still need local participation and ways to refuse or override assistance.

7. An executable audit keeps prevalence and workflow visible

The following standard-library function evaluates a binary alert system from counts. Inputs are nonnegative integers; the data must include at least one positive and one negative case. It returns sensitivity, false-positive rate, positive predictive value, alert rate, and the expected-loss estimate for supplied nonnegative error costs. It stores nothing and performs no network or device operation.

def audit_alerts(tp, fn, fp, tn, miss_cost, alert_cost):
    values = (tp, fn, fp, tn)
    if any(type(value) is not int or value < 0 for value in values):
        raise ValueError("counts must be nonnegative integers")
    if tp + fn == 0 or fp + tn == 0:
        raise ValueError("both outcome classes must be observed")
    if miss_cost < 0 or alert_cost < 0:
        raise ValueError("costs must be nonnegative")
    total = sum(values)
    sensitivity = tp / (tp + fn)
    false_positive_rate = fp / (fp + tn)
    precision = tp / (tp + fp) if tp + fp else 0.0
    alert_rate = (tp + fp) / total
    loss = (fn * miss_cost + fp * alert_cost) / total
    return sensitivity, false_positive_rate, precision, alert_rate, loss


metrics = audit_alerts(9, 1, 49, 931, 100, 1)
expected = (0.9, 0.05, 9 / 58, 58 / 990, 149 / 990)
assert all(abs(left - right) < 1e-12
           for left, right in zip(metrics, expected))

The example has 10 positive and 980 negative intervals. It verifies the earlier sensitivity, false-positive rate, and precision, while reporting that \(58/990\approx5.86\%\) of all intervals create alerts. Its count-based loss is \(149/990\approx0.1505\), which differs from the earlier hypothetical \(0.249\) because the observed miss prevalence is \(1/990\), not \(0.02(0.10)=0.002\). The discrepancy is useful: expected design assumptions and observed evaluation counts must not be blended silently.

This audit cannot measure whether alerts were timely, whether an avoided fall caused another harm, or whether sensing was acceptable to patients. It should be joined to a predeclared evaluation plan, subgroup denominators, privacy attacks, and a workflow study. Executable arithmetic makes assumptions inspectable; it does not make the chosen outcomes sufficient.

8. Exercises with fully worked solutions

  1. Rare-event alerts. A monitor has 95% sensitivity and 98% specificity. For 10,000 intervals with 1% event prevalence, compute the confusion counts and positive predictive value.
  2. Workflow bottleneck. Detection, timely attendance, and prevention probabilities are 0.92, 0.60, and 0.75. Compute coverage. Compare a five-point improvement in detection with a ten-point improvement in attendance.
  3. Aggregate inference. Explain why a precinct-level relation between vehicle mixtures and voting does not identify an individual driver’s vote. State one valid and one invalid use.
  4. Privacy threat models. A depth representation defeats a face classifier but permits 90% gait re-identification. Does it satisfy a claim of identity privacy? Repair the evaluation statement.
  5. Robot safety. A planner reaches its goal at sampled times and reports no collision at those samples. Give a counterexample to continuous-time safety and one operational check.
  6. Human preference. A survey ranks floor cleaning highly and medication delivery moderately. Explain why rank alone cannot determine which robot capability to deploy in a care facility.
Worked solutions

1. There are 100 positive intervals and 9,900 negative intervals. Sensitivity gives 95 true positives and 5 false negatives. Specificity 0.98 gives 198 false positives and 9,702 true negatives. Positive predictive value is \(95/(95+198)=95/293\approx0.324\). Thus roughly two-thirds of alerts are false despite both headline rates being high. Prevalence determines how conditional rates translate into operator workload.

2. Baseline coverage is \(0.92(0.60)(0.75)=0.414\). Raising detection to 0.97 gives \(0.4365\), an increase of 0.0225. Raising attendance to 0.70 gives \(0.483\), an increase of 0.069. Under the product model, workflow improvement has three times the effect. This does not prove it is cheaper or feasible; it identifies which conditional probability limits the stipulated chain.

3. The model relates an aggregate feature vector to an aggregate outcome. Many allocations of individual votes can produce the same precinct total, and car ownership is not uniquely assigned to observed residents. A valid use is estimating a held-out precinct statistic under the study’s sampling conditions, with uncertainty. An invalid use is labeling a photographed driver’s politics. The latter changes the unit of inference and lacks supporting data.

4. It does not satisfy an unqualified identity-privacy claim because gait remains a strong identifier. A precise statement is: under the declared datasets and attacks, the representation reduced face-classification performance to a specified bound but allowed 90% gait re-identification. The system should test multiple attackers, minimize retention, and either reduce motion leakage or narrow its claim. Unknown attacks remain an explicit boundary.

5. A robot link can pass from one side of a thin obstacle to the other between samples while both sampled configurations are collision-free. Check a continuous swept volume or use conservative collision checking over each interpolated segment; alternatively bound velocity and choose a resolution that cannot cross an unchecked clearance. The guarantee depends on the interpolation and geometry, not merely the number of samples.

6. Rank omits clinical risk, technical feasibility, frequency, consent, liability, workflow, and variation among residents. Floor cleaning may be preferred and technically tractable; medication delivery may have greater benefit but much higher error cost. A deployment decision needs task-specific failure analysis, local stakeholder input, refusal and override mechanisms, and evidence from the intended environment. Survey rankings inform task selection without replacing governance.

9. Evidence boundaries and a design discipline 1:04:35

The recording supplies the historical arc, the three “see” perspectives, and examples from fine-grained recognition, population analysis, attention, privacy-preserving sensing, healthcare, open-instruction robotics, and BEHAVIOR. Primary papers establish the specific scale and mechanisms cited here. The hospital alert model, workflow product, privacy-constrained objective, robot safety formalization, code, and exercises are teaching extensions.

Several lecture examples are demonstrations or research prototypes, not evidence of clinical effectiveness or general robot autonomy. The chapter does not endorse continuous surveillance, infer individual traits from regional correlations, or claim that one privacy metric protects every sensitive attribute. Machine transcription supplied timing and contains recognition errors; technical names and numerical claims used here were checked against primary sources.

Human-centered vision is an experimental discipline: specify the people and decision, retain the minimum useful evidence, test the whole intervention, separate outcomes before weighting them, examine who bears failures, and preserve ways to contest or refuse the system. Capability matters. Restraint, workflow, and governance determine whether capability becomes help.
